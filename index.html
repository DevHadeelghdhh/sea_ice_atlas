04The impact

The far south is not far away

What does
absence do to the air?

Ice is a reflective surface. As it retreats, a dark ocean absorbs more solar energy, then releases extra heat and moisture into the atmosphere—especially in autumn.

That may reshape the jet stream, helping weather patterns linger or opening routes for cold polar air into the mid-latitudes. But there is no simple equation: the size of this effect remains under study, alongside natural variability and tropical conditions.

NSIDC connects ice loss to possible regional mechanisms, while noting that the relationship with mid-latitude weather extremes is unresolved. Ice may amplify an existing pattern more than create one on its own.

TakeawaysThree things

What remains after the numbers?

Three
clear takeaways.

01

The low became a pattern

2026 reached the 10th-lowest minimum in the record, and the last 20 years contain the lowest 20 annual minima—the baseline itself is falling.

02

Decades reveal what years hide

The September average fell from 7.21 million km² in 1979–1988 to 4.56 million km² in 2019–2026: a loss of about 36.7%.

03

The impact is not a single arrow

Less ice means more energy exchange between ocean and air, but translating that into weather farther south depends on many factors and remains an active area of research.
